# Dramaturgy and shot design

## Build a scene, not wallpaper

Before writing a shot, define:

`desire + obstacle + space geometry + controlled gaze + editing rhythm`

- Desire: what the subject wants in this moment.
- Obstacle: what physically or psychologically prevents it.
- Geometry: power position, threat, exit, direction of movement.
- Gaze: the one place the viewer should look first.
- Rhythm: duration, pause, and cut logic.

If the request is non-narrative, translate the same structure: product/subject goal, friction or transformation, spatial demonstration, focal hierarchy, and reveal rhythm.

## Give the shot a reason to move

A strong short clip contains a visible change, not merely a moving camera. Define:

- initial state;
- pressure or trigger from the environment;
- subject response or micro-action;
- final state, reveal, or unresolved hook.

At minimum, add one environmental force, one small body/object response, and one sound or recurring visual motif when audio is supported. These details create causal motion and keep the scene from feeling like a static image being animated.

Every shot must also change emotion, advance action/information, or increase pressure. A beauty shot must reveal, prove, transform, or resolve something to earn its place.

## Control information over time

Use chronology when sequence matters:

1. Establish the readable starting state.
2. Introduce one principal action or transformation.
3. Let the camera reveal, follow, or reframe that action.
4. End on a deliberate composition that can cut, loop, or hand off to the next shot.

Do not overload a short duration. One principal subject action and one principal camera move are usually enough. Add secondary movement only when it reinforces the primary action.

Cut in this priority order: emotional truth, story/information, rhythm, eye trace, screen direction, then physical geography. Speed alone is not a reason to cut.

## Choose shot grammar by purpose

- Establishing wide: geography, scale, atmosphere.
- Medium tracking: readable body action and context.
- Close-up or macro: material, expression, product detail.
- Push-in: attention, tension, importance.
- Pull-out: reveal, isolation, expanded context.
- Orbit: form and dimensionality; risky for identity and geometry.
- Locked frame: graphic composition, transformation, performance.

Specify only motions that fit the available duration. Prefer one primary camera move with an explicit speed and end state.

Every camera move answers `what changed?`: a decision, new information, increasing pressure, a gaze reveal, an entering subject, or a shift in space. Otherwise lock the camera.

## Plan a multi-shot arc

Give each shot a distinct job: hook, establish, demonstrate, escalate, reveal, proof, or resolve. Maintain screen direction and visual anchors across cuts. If the mode supports timestamped multi-shot generation, express cuts and handoffs inside the generation prompt; otherwise put transition intent in node purposes and an ordered sequence specification.

## Five-anchor check

Name exactly one primary emotion, recurring motif, anchor object, break/turn, and final image. Run these checks before handing off a prompt:

1. Is the scene formula complete?
2. Does every shot carry environmental pressure, physical micro-action, and sound/motif?
3. Does every shot change emotion, advance action/information, or increase pressure?
4. Is every camera move motivated?
5. Is spatial geometry readable?
6. Are the five anchors explicit?
